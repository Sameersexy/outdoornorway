# Craft: decision tree, frameworks and the edit

What it is: the craft playbook behind every adquill ad.

Read it when you diagnose a brief (method step 3), pick angles (step 4), write to slots (step 5) or edit (step 6). Hooks, headlines and CTAs have their own file (hooks-and-headlines.md). Offer, risk and price are in offer-and-price.md.

All examples use one invented company, Brekke Ski Guides. Every example is a fictional brief. Names, prices, dates, group sizes and policies are invented and carry no claim about any real operator.

## 1. How to read the evidence tags

Craft doctrine is mostly working knowledge handed down by practitioners. Very little of it has been tested in controlled studies. adquill tags every claim about a book, a person or a study so you can see how far to trust it.

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| [PRIMARY] | The original text or the platform's own page was read by the research team. For a book, this means the cited chapters, not necessarily the whole book. |
| [SECONDARY] | A summary, review or teaching page was read. The idea is probably right. The exact wording is not guaranteed. |
| [VERIFY: reason] | A number or claim that could not be confirmed. Do not put it in an ad. Do not quote it in public. |

Rules for you when you write with this file:

- Never quote a [SECONDARY] line as if you had read the book.
- Never put a [VERIFY] number into an ad.
- The only numbers that go into an ad are the ones in the fact ledger, because the user supplied them and can check them.

## 2. The decision tree

Two questions decide most of the copy. How aware is the reader? How many similar claims has the market already heard? The model comes from Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966) [SECONDARY: summaries only, the book is in copyright]. Treat it as a diagnosis and not a template.

Schwartz held that desire already exists in the market, and that copy can only focus it on a product [SECONDARY, VERIFY exact wording: https://mirasee.com/blog/eugene-schwartz-breakthrough-advertising/]. Take the practical meaning. Find the want that is already there. Do not invent one.

### 2.1 The inputs

1. Awareness level of the audience for this ad. Defaults to problem aware if the user gives nothing.
2. Sophistication stage of the market. Defaults to stage 3 if the user gives nothing.
3. The fact ledger. Anything missing becomes a question to the user, never a guess.

Inference rule for awareness (adquill's working rule, not a Schwartz rule): cold prospecting audiences are mostly unaware or problem aware. People who visited the site are product aware. People who started a booking, or who are on the email list, are most aware.

Inference rule for stage: look at what competitors actually run. The public Meta Ad Library lists live ads. If the user cannot say which stage the market sits in, offer to read ten competitor ads first and say what you saw. Do not guess a stage and present it as a finding.

### 2.2 Awareness level decides the subject

| Level | What the reader knows | Lead with | Framework | Preferred hook types | Static image line | Reel hook | X post |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Nothing wrong, not looking | An identity, a scene or a surprise. Do not name the product first | BAB, story | Pattern interrupt, story | A scene or identity, price last | Start mid-scene, text overlay | One vivid scene, then the number |
| Problem aware | Has the want or the pain, thinks no fix exists | The situation in their own words, a hint it is solvable | PAS (light), BAB | Question, direct benefit | "Never X? Start with Y." | Name the situation in 3 seconds | A question, then one fact |
| Solution aware | Knows the type of solution, not your product | Your mechanism and why it differs | QUEST (qualify first), FAB (publish the benefit first) | Specificity, mechanism, curiosity paid off | The named mechanism plus a number | Show the mechanism | Line one mechanism, line two proof |
| Product aware | Knows you, not yet sure | Proof, the honest concession, risk reducer | Proof stack, risk reversal, 4Ps (promise and proof) | Proof, concession, specificity | Proof as headline (date, count, policy) | Real footage and a real number | Proof line, then link |
| Most aware | Wants it, needs a reason to decide this week | The offer, the date, the price | Offer first, 4Ps push | Direct benefit, specificity | Date plus price | Deal on screen at half a second | Date list plus link |

Sources for the table: Schwartz levels from the summaries above and https://selzee.com/eugene-schwartz-5-levels-of-awareness [SECONDARY]. Summaries disagree on level names. This is the common five. The hook and framework columns are adquill's synthesis of the canon, not a published standard.

### 2.3 Sophistication stage adjusts the claim

| Stage | Market condition | What wins | Plain example in ski touring (fictional brief) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nobody has made the claim | Say the claim plainly | "Guided ski touring in the Sunnmore Alps." |
| 2 | Others copy it | Enlarge the claim with a bigger or tighter number | "Six guests per guide. Never seven." |
| 3 | Claims are tired | Add a mechanism, the how | "Saturday you learn the kit. Sunday you use it." |
| 4 | Mechanisms are copied | Sharpen the mechanism, add proof, name the difference from the usual | "Our route decision is made at 6 a.m. from the avalanche forecast, and we publish it." |
| 5 | Everything has been heard | Identity and belonging | "For people who would rather earn the turn." |

Stage sources: https://www.selfstorming.com/tools/libraries/frameworks/market-sophistication and https://auresnotes.com/summary-breakthrough-advertising-eugene-schwartz/ [SECONDARY]. Hopkins already shows the pattern. When every soap maker said "abundant lather", one maker said it "multiplies itself in lather 250 times" [PRIMARY: Scientific Advertising, chapter 7, https://archive.org/details/scientific-advertising]. That is stage 2 to 3: enlarge the claim and make it specific.

### 2.4 Both answers together

Awareness picks the subject: what the line is about. Stage picks how far the claim must go before anyone believes it. Read the table by row (awareness) and column (stage). Each cell is the instruction for the line. adquill's synthesis, not a published standard. Examples are fictional brief.

| Awareness | Stage 1: plain claim | Stage 2: bigger claim | Stage 3: mechanism | Stage 4: sharper mechanism | Stage 5: identity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | A scene of the activity, offer in line two | The scene plus one tighter number | A scene that shows the mechanism at work | A scene of what the usual trip skips | One identity line in the buyer's own words |
| Problem aware | Name the want, state the offer plainly | Name the want, enlarge with a number | Name the want, then the mechanism that answers it | Name the want, show why the usual fix misses it | Name the want as a shared want: "for people who..." |
| Solution aware | Say what, where, when, how much | The same with the tighter number (six per guide) | Lead with the mechanism, number second | Lead with the sharper mechanism and proof it runs | Lead with the stance, the mechanism is the proof |
| Product aware | Remove the one doubt with one fact | Remove it with a number the reader can check | Show the mechanism running (the real forecast, the real route plan) | Proof stack: a real review, a real count, a real policy | Proof that the right people already go: a real quote, with permission |
| Most aware | Date and price | Date, price and the tighter number | Date, price and the named structure | Date, price, one proof line, one real risk reducer | Date, price, one line in the tribe's voice |

Two rules for using it.

1. When two cells seem to fit, take the one with fewer claims.
2. When you cannot tell the row or the column, ask once. Ask who sees the ad (strangers, site visitors, people who started to book) and ask the user to paste three competitor ads from the Meta Ad Library. Do not guess a stage and present it as a finding.

### 2.5 Then run the building blocks

After awareness and stage, check the nine blocks below on every draft. They are covered in the sections that follow.

1. One idea.
2. A reason.
3. A specific number, date or named thing.
4. A mechanism, if the market is at stage 3 or above.
5. A link to the campaign's big idea.
6. A clean offer (offer-and-price.md).
7. A risk reducer, only if a real one exists.
8. A price shown with the right frame.
9. A plain next step (hooks-and-headlines.md, CTA section).

Then score the headline (hooks-and-headlines.md), edit (section 11), count characters in code, check every number against the fact ledger, and label the ad with its receipt.

## 3. Worked cases (fictional brief)

Brief for the cases. Brekke Ski Guides is a small invented guiding company in the Sunnmore Alps. Invented ledger: day tour NOK 2,350 per person, guided Saturdays from January to April 2027, six guests at most per guide, avalanche kit lent. Weekend course NOK 4,900. Six day hut-to-hut trip NOK 18,500, two Sunday departures (14 and 21 Mar 2027), eight guests, two guides, five to seven hours a day on skins. Everything here is invented to show method.

Each case shows the reasoning table first, then the lines. Character counts were checked in code.

### Case A: cold audience, day tour

| Awareness | Stage | Framework | Hook type | Proof fact | Source in ledger |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Problem aware | 3 | BAB | Question, identification | Six guests at most | day tour line, group cap line |

- Image line: "Monday: inbox. Saturday: a ridge above the fjord. NOK 2,350." (60 characters)
- Primary text: "Never skied a mountain without a lift? One guided Saturday, six guests at most. NOK 2,350." (90 characters)
- Why it works: it hails a person who has resort skills and no mountain experience. It gives the exact schedule, the cap and the price in the first 125 characters. Hopkins wrote the rule in 1923: the first thing you say is "Hey there, Bill Jones" to get the right person's attention [PRIMARY: chapter 5].

### Case B: solution aware, weekend course

| Awareness | Stage | Framework | Hook type | Proof fact | Source in ledger |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solution aware | 3 | QUEST step one, then FAB | Specificity, curiosity paid off | Day order of the course | weekend course line |

- Image line: "Saturday you learn it. Sunday you use it. NOK 4,900." (52 characters)
- Primary text: "Saturday: practise the avalanche kit on a slope we have checked. Sunday: use it on a summit you choose." (103 characters)
- Why it works: the structure of the two days is the mechanism. The curiosity in the image line is paid off in the first line of the text. There is no promise about conditions.

### Case C: product aware, six day trip

| Awareness | Stage | Framework | Hook type | Proof fact | Source in ledger |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Product aware | 4 | Proof stack, risk reversal | Proof, honest concession | Eight guests, two guides | six day trip line, policy line |

- Image line: "Six days. Two departures. 14 and 21 Mar 2027. NOK 18,500." (57 characters)
- Primary text: "Six days on skins, hut to hut. 8 guests, 2 guides. Five to seven hours uphill a day. Hard work." (95 characters)
- Add only with a real policy in the ledger: one line about what happens if the avalanche forecast closes a route. In this fictional brief the invented policy reads "If the forecast closes a route, the guide picks another. If we cancel, you get a full refund."
- Why it works: the concession (hard work) screens out the wrong buyer and makes the rest believable. Bernbach's agency used the same move for Volkswagen in 1960 [SECONDARY, see section 10].

### Case D: most aware, departure dates

| Awareness | Stage | Framework | Hook type | Proof fact | Source in ledger |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Most aware | 3 | Offer first | Direct benefit, specificity | Two real departure dates | six day trip line |

- Image line: "Two departures: 14 and 21 Mar 2027. Six days. NOK 18,500." (57 characters)
- Primary text: "Two departures: 14 and 21 Mar 2027. 6 days, hut to hut. NOK 18,500 a person." (76 characters)
- Why it works: no persuasion is left to do. The reader has decided and needs date and price. "Two departures" is true because the ledger lists two. It is scarcity that is real because it is a count of dates and not a countdown.

Self-check on the four cases. Cases C and D have the same three-beat shape. That is fine for a data-led offer. In an emotional ad it would read as a machine. Vary the shape between ads in the same campaign.

## 4. Frameworks that survive scrutiny

Most formulas were built for long copy: sales pages, letters, video scripts. In a short headline or a 125 character hook they shrink to a checklist and stop being a structure. The honest use: pick the formula to decide what to include, then cut until one idea is left. Do not show formula names to the user as a menu. They are hidden structure.

Definitions for the formulas below come from the Copyhackers formula guide [PRIMARY for the definitions: https://copyhackers.com/2015/10/copywriting-formula/]. The linked example pages in that guide were not opened, so adquill describes none of them.

| Formula | What it is | Use it for | Where it fails | Origin, stated honestly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AIDA | Attention, Interest, Desire, Action | An order check on a landing page | Feeds, where attention, desire and action can all happen in two seconds. It also assumes a straight line from awareness to purchase, which research has not supported | Contested. An unsigned Printers' Ink item of 9 February 1898 already described the idea. E. St. Elmo Lewis is the usual name, with a 1910 line. The acronym appears in print by 1921 [SECONDARY: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIDA_(marketing)] |
| PAS | Problem, Agitate, Solve | Problem aware audiences, a Reel script (problem in 3 seconds, agitation in the next 5, fix after) | Aspirational products. A ski trip is a treat, and heavy agitation sounds wrong. Meta also restricts copy that implies a personal attribute (see policy.md) | Dan Kennedy is credited with calling it "the most reliable sales formula ever invented". That shows he praised it. It does not show he invented it [SECONDARY: http://www.nonprofitcopywriter.com/PAS-copywriting-formula.html, VERIFY the date of origin] |
| BAB | Before, After, Bridge | Aspiration and travel, a six second video, a split frame image | When the "after" cannot be shown honestly | The research found no first author [SECONDARY: https://dowordsgood.com/before-after-bridge-formula-ad-trick/, VERIFY] |
| 4Ps | Two competing versions. Picture, Promise, Prove, Push (credited to Henry Hoke Sr.). Problem, Promise, Proof, Proposal (credited to Ray Edwards) | The two versions agree on the middle: promise, then proof | Sources mix the two. Say which one you mean | [SECONDARY: https://copyhackers.com/2015/10/copywriting-formula/ and https://copywriting.school/formulas/pppp-picture-promise-prove-push, VERIFY the Hoke attribution] |
| 4Us | Useful, Urgent, Unique, Ultra-specific | Ranking 30 headlines fast. It is the base of the scorecard in hooks-and-headlines.md | When "urgent" is faked to score points | Credited to Michael Masterson and the AWAI school [SECONDARY: https://www.awai.com/2022/05/4-goof-proof-ways-to-write-irresistible-headlines/] |
| PASTOR | Problem, Amplify, Story, Transformation, Offer, Response | Sales pages, email, a 60 second video, long threads | Anything under 100 words. Do not force it onto a 125 character hook | Ray Edwards [SECONDARY: https://www.shortform.com/blog/ray-edwards-p-a-s-t-o-r-framework/] |
| QUEST | Qualify, Understand, Educate, Stimulate, Transition | Reel scripts of 20 to 30 seconds. Step one is Hopkins' "Hey there, Bill Jones" | The educate step needs room | Attributed to Michel Fortin [SECONDARY: search summary, page not opened, VERIFY] |
| FAB | Feature, Advantage, Benefit | Turning a spec into a headline. Write all three, publish the benefit on the image, the advantage as the second line, the feature as proof | Copy that stops at the feature | No clear originator [SECONDARY: https://copyhackers.com/2015/10/copywriting-formula/] |

A worked FAB for a Brekke line (fictional brief). Feature: six guests per guide. Advantage: the guide can see every skier on every ascent. Benefit: nobody is left behind on a slope that can hurt them. Publish the benefit and the number: "Six guests. One guide. Nobody left behind." Keep the feature as the proof line.

The rule for all of them: a formula never lengthens an ad.

## 5. One idea per ad, and the full story

Two famous pieces of advice point in opposite directions.

- Hopkins: "Bring all your good arguments to bear". He also says the average reader is only once a reader, so every ad must carry the whole case [PRIMARY: Scientific Advertising, chapter 8, https://archive.org/details/scientific-advertising].
- Dave Trott: create a single message that people believe [SECONDARY, VERIFY the wording: https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/25311444-predatory-thinking-a-masterclass-in-out-thinking-the-competition].

Hopkins wrote for one newspaper insertion that a reader met once and had time to read. A feed gives you a glance, and it lets you show the same person many ads. Current guides say Meta's delivery rewards different concepts and treats near-copies as one [SECONDARY: https://www.dentsu.com/ae/en/our-latest-thinking/meta-andromeda-strategy, VERIFY against Meta's own statement]. Vendor numbers on how many concepts to run are untraced [VERIFY].

The reconciliation adquill applies:

1. One idea per ad.
2. Many different ideas per campaign.
3. The full story lives on the landing page, in the long primary text and in the FAQ.

How to apply it:

- The one-sentence test. Show the ad for two seconds, then ask the viewer to describe it in one sentence. If they need two sentences, there are two ads in it. Luke Sullivan's advice to reduce an ad to one element supports the test [SECONDARY, thin: https://www.befreed.ai/book/hey-whipple-squeeze-this-by-luke-sullivan-with-sam-bennett].
- The claim count. List the claims in the ad. More than one means split it. "Six guests per guide" and "avalanche kit lent" are two claims and two ads.
- The campaign test. Two ads that sell the same claim in different words are one idea. Variants must differ in angle, hook type and proof, as defined in SKILL.md.

Example of a split (fictional brief). Draft: "Six guests per guide. Kit lent. Two departures. NOK 18,500." That is four claims. Split it into four ads, one per claim, each with its own image and its own headline. The landing page carries all four.

## 6. Specificity

Replace the general with the exact. Hopkins gives the rule: platitudes and generalities "roll off the human understanding like water from a duck", and "a man who makes a specific claim is either telling the truth or a lie". Say a lamp gives more light and people doubt it. Say it gives three and one-third times the light and they believe you made a test [PRIMARY: chapter 7, https://archive.org/details/scientific-advertising].

Harry Dry's three tests turn the rule into a check [SECONDARY summary of his course: https://www.upgrow.io/harry-dry-copywriting-3-rules/]:

1. Can you picture it?
2. Can you falsify it?
3. Can nobody else say it?

Dry's tips page is the primary source for his rules on eraser editing, adjectives and "landing page words" [PRIMARY: https://marketingexamples.com/copywriting/tips]. His site is a teaching product. Some of its numbers are his own or retold from social posts. Quote the rules, never the revenue figures [VERIFY].

| Expert guides | Two guides for eight guests | A count instead of a label |
| Great mountain views | You see Hjorundfjord from the ridge at about 1,400 m | A named place and a height. Needs a ledger source |
| Easy to book | Pick a date, pay a deposit, done in three taps | A visible step count. Needs a ledger source |

Two warnings.

- A number that does not matter is noise. "37 peaks" says nothing if the reader cares about their own day.
- A number that is wrong is worse than none. Check every number against the ledger.

Nearly every specific the skill writes must trace to a ledger line. If the user has no number, write the honest vague line and ask for the number. Do not invent one to look sharp.

## 7. Reason why

Give a reason with every claim. The credit for reason-why copy goes to the advertising writer John E. Kennedy, and Hopkins pressed copywriters to research the product until they found real reasons [SECONDARY: https://www.igi-global.com/dictionary/claude-hopkins/74465, VERIFY].

The evidence people usually cite is Langer, Blank and Chanowitz (1978), the photocopier study [SECONDARY: https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/power-of-because-langer-1978/, 120 adults, VERIFY against the paper]. A person asks to cut a queue at a copier.

| Request | No reason | Real reason ("I am in a rush") | Empty reason ("because I have to make copies") |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copy 5 pages | 60% comply | 94% | 93% |
| Copy 20 pages | about 24% | 42% | about 24% |

Take the right lesson. On a small ask, any "because" helps. On a larger ask, only a real reason helps. A NOK 18,500 trip is a larger ask. So the reason must be real, and it should be a fact the reader can check. Gary Bencivenga's jury test sets the bar: what proof would persuade a fair-minded jury beyond reasonable doubt [SECONDARY, VERIFY wording: https://gameofconversions.com/gary-bencivenga-shoestring-business-ad-the-bencivenga-persuasion-equation/].

Reason-why in a short slot: put the number in the line, because the number is the reason.

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->
- Weak (fictional brief): "We keep our groups small for a better experience."
<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-end -->
- Strong: "Six guests per guide, so the guide sees every skier on every climb."
- Why: the strong line gives the cause and the number. The weak line gives a conclusion with nothing under it.

## 8. Mechanism

The mechanism is the how. It explains why this product delivers when others did not. Schwartz puts it at stage 3 and above [SECONDARY, see section 2].

Hopkins gives the cleanest case. When every rival brewer shouted that the product was pure, one described the process: a plate glass cooling room, filtered air, bottles "washed four times by machinery", water from 4,000 feet, and 1,018 experiments to find the yeast [PRIMARY for the text: chapter 7]. The text does not name the brewer. Teaching sources name Schlitz and claim sales rose from fifth place to a tie for first. The research could not confirm either [SECONDARY: search summary, VERIFY the name and the rank claim].

Three rules for a mechanism:

1. It must be true. A made-up mechanism is a false claim, and the skill refuses to write one.
2. It must be visible. A reader should be able to imagine it, or the image should show it.
3. A name is free mechanism copy. The structure of a trip, the order of the days or a routine can carry a name that sticks.

Fictional brief examples:

- Structure as mechanism: "Saturday you learn it. Sunday you use it."
- Process as mechanism: "The guide reads the avalanche forecast at 6 a.m. and picks the route. The group does not vote."
- Constraint as mechanism: "Eight guests, two guides, one hut a night. That is the whole trip."

If the user has no real mechanism, say so. Offer the next best thing, a specific fact or an honest concession, and do not dress up the ordinary as a system.

## 9. Big idea

A big idea is one concept that can produce many ads. Ogilvy and Bernbach both used the term [SECONDARY]. A working test: can the idea be written as a headline, a Reel script and an X post without changing its meaning? If it cannot, it is a slogan.

The example that gets cited most is Avis, "We try harder" (1962, copywriter Paula Green with art director Helmut Krone). It produced lines such as "Avis can't afford dirty ashtrays". Avis moved from a loss of $3.2 million to a profit of $1.2 million in the first year, and the gap with Hertz narrowed between 1963 and 1966 [SECONDARY: https://slate.com/business/2013/08/hertz-vs-avis-advertising-wars-how-an-ad-firm-made-a-virtue-out-of-second-place.html]. The cause of the profit swing was not isolated from other factors. The claim that the tagline ran for about fifty years is a search summary [VERIFY].

adquill's rule: write the big idea once per campaign, in one sentence, and test every ad against it. Fictional brief: "We take you up the mountain and we tell you the truth about it." Ads under that idea: the concession ad, the forecast-at-6-a.m. ad, the six-guests ad.

Small operators do not need a famous tagline. They need one repeatable stance, so strangers can recognise the next ad as the same company.

## 10. The honest concession

The strongest use of Bernbach's school is a true weakness turned into a signal. Bill Bernbach's agency, DDB, paired a copywriter and an art director in one room. He called advertising persuasion and called persuasion an art [SECONDARY: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Bernbach].

The Volkswagen "Lemon" ad (1960) is the model. One word headline. The copy tells you one chrome strip on the glove compartment was blemished, that inspector Kurt Kroner caught it, that 3,389 men inspect cars at the Wolfsburg factory and say no to one car in fifty. It ends: "We pluck the lemons; you get the plums." [SECONDARY: transcription via https://swipefile.com/volkswagen-lemon-print-ad/lemon-ad, VERIFY against a scan before quoting]. Note the structure: a small admitted flaw, specific numbers, a twist in the last line. "Think Small" (1959, Julian Koenig and Helmut Krone) is credited as the best campaign of the 20th century by Advertising Age in 1999 [SECONDARY: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Think_Small].

Rules for using a concession:

1. The concession must be true. Never fake an admission.
2. It must be backed by a real strength in the same ad or on the landing page.
3. It should filter. A concession that screens out the wrong buyer saves money and raises trust.
4. It should be specific. "Not for everyone" says nothing. "Five to seven hours a day uphill" says plenty.

Fictional brief:

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->
- Weak: "Challenging but rewarding!"
<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-end -->
- Strong: "Five to seven hours a day on skins. If that sounds miserable, take the weekend course."
- Why: it names the cost in hours, it screens, and it sends the unsuited reader to a cheaper product that suits them.

## 11. How senior copywriters edit

Most of what follows is working doctrine. The sources agree on direction: write many, cut hard, check facts, test. No source agrees on the right count. Resist adding ritual.

### 11.1 Write many, pick few

Hopkins says a writer "often spends hours on a single headline" and discards scores of them before picking [PRIMARY: chapter 5]. Ogilvy is quoted as writing at least sixteen headlines for a single ad, and a summary says 26 for the Rolls-Royce ad [SECONDARY: quote pages, VERIFY]. The counts 16, 26, 50 and 100 are conventions. adquill uses a pool, then a cut (hooks-and-headlines.md has the 25 line procedure).

The Rolls-Royce line itself: "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock." The line came from reading about the car for about three weeks and from a quote by the Technical Editor of The Motor [SECONDARY: https://www.enchantingmarketing.com/david-ogilvy-rolls-royce-advert/]. Dates differ by source (1957, 1958, 1959) [VERIFY]. Note what it does. It never says "quiet". A fact implies the benefit.

### 11.2 Cut with rules, not feelings

Orwell's rules are the best edit checklist ever written, and the text is open [PRIMARY: https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/]:

1. Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

### 11.3 Kill adjectives, keep facts

Hopkins: superlatives "suggest looseness of expression, a tendency to exaggerate" [PRIMARY: chapter 7]. Dry softens it: "Adjectives are okay. Just ideas don't start with them." [PRIMARY: https://marketingexamples.com/copywriting/adjectives].

The edit pass: underline every adjective. Next to each, write the number, name, date or object behind it. Replace the adjective with that. If nothing sits behind it, cut the word.

Fictional brief: "An epic backcountry adventure in stunning mountains" becomes "One Saturday, six guests, a summit at 1,400 m. NOK 2,350." (the height is an invented ledger line).

### 11.4 Read it aloud

There is no primary source that proves reading aloud works. It is craft convention and it fits Dry's "write how you talk" and David Abbott's plain, simple, familiar words [SECONDARY: https://briefercopy.com/blog/david-abbott/]. Machine version: vary sentence length, and flag three beats of equal length in a row. Dry praises the tricolon ("One scoop. Once a day. Every day.") [PRIMARY: https://marketingexamples.com/copywriting/rhetorical-devices]. In adquill a tricolon is allowed only when the content really has three items.

### 11.5 Walk away, then use a fresh reader

Abbott: the best thing when tired is to do something else [SECONDARY: https://briefercopy.com/blog/david-abbott/]. In a pipeline, the equivalent is a reader who sees only the draft and the brief. That is why the panel command runs separate reviewers.

### 11.6 Test the right unit

Hopkins keyed coupons so every headline's returns were known, and reports that the same ad with different headlines "differs tremendously in its returns" [PRIMARY: chapter 5]. Caples built a method on testing too.

On Meta the unit worth testing is the concept: the angle and the hook type. A test of one adjective against another teaches nothing. Small budgets cannot detect small differences, so test big contrasts. Hook rate (3-second plays divided by impressions) is a ratio you compute from numbers Meta shows. Vendor guides call 30 to 40 percent average for Reels. Those are vendor figures and not Meta benchmarks [SECONDARY: https://reloop.so/blog/article/hook-rate/, VERIFY].

### 11.7 The fact pass

Every number, date and price is checked against the ledger. Amateurs skip this step and it is the one that gets ads rejected. See policy.md.

### 11.8 The promise test

Dry: a good title is one where the reader would feel cheated if the content broke the promise [PRIMARY: https://marketingexamples.com/copywriting/promise]. Apply it to every curiosity hook. If the next line does not pay the curiosity off, rewrite the hook.

### 11.9 The adquill edit loop

1. Draft 30 headlines across the hook types that fit the awareness level.
2. Delete any line with a banned word, a dash, an emoji, a stock contrast frame or an unverified fact.
3. Score the rest with the headline scorecard.
4. Pick five that differ in hook type. Rewrite each with Orwell rules 2, 3 and 4.
5. Count characters in code. Check every number against the ledger.
6. Run the linter and the fresh reader.
7. Ship with the receipt: angle, mechanism, risk, test hypothesis.

## 12. System checks from the modern evidence

Five modern sources work better as system checks across a campaign than as rules for one ad. They set the mix and the repeated cues. None of them says how to write line one.

1. Wiebe, voice of customer. No headline without a customer phrase, or a stated reason none exists. Method: read 200 or more reviews, extract the language into a table, find recurring themes, then test [PRIMARY: https://copyhackers.com/2014/10/amazon-review-mining/]. The best known result is a single client case from Copyhackers' own report. A control headline "Your Addiction Ends Here" lost to a line lifted from a reviewer, "If you think you need rehab, you do", with more than 400 percent more clicks on the button and more than 20 percent more form submissions. One case, reported by the party who ran it. Never invent a guest phrase. If the user has no reviews, say so.
2. Dry, the three tests (section 6).
3. Hormozi, the value equation. Dream outcome times perceived likelihood, divided by time delay times effort. List which of the four levers the ad moves [SECONDARY: https://www.selfstorming.com/tools/libraries/frameworks/value-equation and https://www.scalabl.com/literature/100m-offers/]. It is a thinking model, not measured science. Details in offer-and-price.md.
4. Sharp, *How Brands Grow* (2010): reach many buyers and be easy to remember and easy to buy, through distinctive assets such as colour, shape and a repeated phrase [SECONDARY: https://www.yourmarketingrules.com/how-brands-grow/, VERIFY the wording]. The research draws on large repeat purchase categories and is debated for rare purchases such as a ski trip [VERIFY: scope]. Use it narrowly: pick a few brand cues and place them the same way in every ad.
5. Binet and Field, *The Long and the Short of It* (2013). In the IPA databank, rational messages give a big direct effect that decays quickly, and emotional priming decays slowly with a smaller sales effect [PRIMARY: slides at https://ipa.co.uk/media/5811/long_and_short_of_it_presentation_final.pdf]. The data come from award entries by large, mostly UK brands. They do not tell a small operator how to write line one. The use that survives: a campaign needs both rational ads (price, date) and emotional ads (scene, story), not only one kind.

## 13. When to break these rules

- Orwell's sixth rule covers it. Break any rule sooner than say something outright barbarous.
- When the audience is the buyer's friend and not the buyer (a group chat line), plain warmth beats the discipline of the tree.
- When a user brings a brand voice that contradicts the defaults, follow the user's voice and keep the honesty rules. Honesty is the one set of rules that never bends.
- When a user wants a claim you cannot support, write the ad without it and say why. Do not write the claim and hope.
