# Psychology to copy: 40 mechanisms, graded, with an example line and a misuse risk

What it is: the lookup table that turns buyer psychology into copy moves, with an evidence grade, a platform fit and a misuse risk for each. Read it at method step 4 when you name the mechanism and the risk for each angle, and again at step 7 when you check that no lever crosses the ethics rules in section 5.

Every example line uses the invented company Brekke Ski Guides. Every example is a fictional brief. Prices, dates, group sizes, policies and inclusions are invented for teaching and carry no claim about any real operator. Where a line needs proof (a review, a rating, a certification), the example shows a bracketed placeholder. The real skill fills it from a fact ledger row and writes nothing if the row is empty.

The invented Brekke ledger used below: guided day tour NOK 2,350 per person on Saturdays from January to April 2027, six guests per guide, avalanche kit lent. Weekend course NOK 4,900. Six day hut-to-hut trip NOK 18,500 per person, two Sunday departures (14 and 21 Mar 2027), eight guests, two guides, five to seven hours a day on skins. Deposit NOK 3,000, balance due 60 days before departure, full refund if the guest cancels 60 days or more before departure. Booking for 14 Mar closes 1 Feb 2027, when the huts are paid for.

## 1. How to use this file

Pick one lead mechanism and one support per ad. Never stack more. An ad that tries social proof, scarcity, free, authority and curiosity in 125 characters reads as noise and trips the policy checks. The receipt names the lead only, plus the risk you checked.

Prefer the higher grade when two levers fit. Google's simulation of 31,000 online shoppers found social proof had the largest or second-largest effect in 28 of 31 categories, and scarcity was most often the weakest of six levers [Google and The Behavioural Architects, 2020: https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/9998/Decoding_Decisions_The_Messy_Middle_of_Purchase_Behavior.pdf]. That result comes from UK shoppers, simulated purchases and fictional brands, so treat it as a strong prior and not as a law for your audience.

Name the mechanism with its grade in the receipt, for example "social proof: specific review (grade B)". Never cite research in the copy or the notes unless you can print the source.

Rotate levers across a test set. If every variant leans on the same mechanism, the test shows nothing about which one works for this audience.

Match the lever to the buyer's stage. Trigger-stage readers need a scene (rows 21 to 27). Doubt-stage readers need risk answers (rows 15, 16, 33). Decision-stage readers need a small next step (rows 11, 12, 38). The stage list is in references/buyer-journey-and-anxieties.md.

## 2. Evidence grades

| Grade | Meaning in this file |
|---|---|
| A | Peer reviewed and replicated, or covered by a meta-analysis that tests the copy move itself |
| B | Large dataset, platform-official data, or peer reviewed work with a consistent direction whose replication the research could not confirm |
| C | Practitioner lore, a model with little formal test, or theory with no direct ad evidence |

The grade rates the move as used in ad copy. It does not grade the theory behind it. No row earns an A today. Applied ad tests rarely come with the replication the grade needs, and the notes could not confirm replications for the classic studies. A contributor can promote a row by adding a replication or meta-analysis URL to the basis notes.

Where a row says [VERIFY: reason], the claim could not be confirmed from the primary source when the notes were written (2026-10-06).

## 4. Basis notes for the table

This section says why each grade sits where it does. Add a replication or meta-analysis URL here to promote a row.

Rows 1, 2, 4, 5, 9, 18, 19: Google's simulation (31,000 online shoppers, 310,000 simulated purchases, 31 categories, UK, July 2020). Social proof largest or second-largest in 28 of 31 categories. Power of free largest or second-largest in 18 of 31. Authority smaller, strongest where buyers lack domain knowledge, and impartial publications outweighed industry ones. Scarcity most often the least effective, and for shoppers still exploring it "could feel restrictive and provoke a negative reaction". Source: https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/9998/Decoding_Decisions_The_Messy_Middle_of_Purchase_Behavior.pdf. Dan Ariely co-authored the original zero-price paper and has had other work retracted (see section 6). Cite Google's test for row 9 and never write "Ariely found".

Row 3: Goldstein, Cialdini and Griskevicius ran hotel field experiments. A towel-reuse sign citing what most guests do beat a standard environmental appeal, and citing guests "in this room" added a further gain [Journal of Consumer Research 35(3): https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1086/586910]. Peer reviewed field work, no replication checked, so grade B at most.

Rows 6, 7: Cialdini's liking and unity. Unity came in the 2021 expanded edition of Influence [overview: https://www.neurosciencemarketing.com/blog/articles/cialdini-7th-unity.htm]. No ad-specific test found. Grade C.

Row 8: Practitioner reading. Among popular open source skill repos, the ones that state their own limits in the README (a small sample size, a benchmark caveat) read as more credible. That is an observation with no controlled test behind it [VERIFY: no URL for a controlled study]. Grade C.

Rows 10, 11: Cialdini's reciprocity and commitment. Practitioner translation. Grade C.

Row 12: Prelec and Loewenstein (1998) model a "pain of paying" and predict people like to prepay so that consumption feels free [Marketing Science 17(1): https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.17.1.4]. Fogg's money factor pushes the other way at first contact, so a small deposit lowers the barrier. Both can hold. Grade C for the copy move.

Row 13: Tversky and Kahneman (1974) anchoring [Science 185: https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124]. Effects in real price settings are smaller than lab numbers, and buyers with more product knowledge shift less [price anchoring review: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7434854/]. Intermediate and expert skiers will check your number.

Row 14: Gourville (1998) showed "pennies a day" framing raises compliance by moving the comparison to small recurring expenses [Journal of Consumer Research 24(4): https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/24/4/395/1797969]. The effect needs a plausible small-expense comparison. Norwegian price rules expect the total for package travel [https://lovdata.no/artikkel/pakkereiser/5166] [VERIFY with counsel].

Row 15: Hiding mandatory fees until checkout raised revenue by about 21 percent on StubHub, and buyers were 14.1 percent more likely to complete [Blake, Moshary, Sweeney and Tadelis, Marketing Science 40(4), 2021: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25186/w25186.pdf]. That dataset explains why drip pricing tempts, and why regulators target it. The claim that showing everything up front earns trust has no direct test in the notes. Grade C.

Row 16: Vendor case studies only. An Expedia partner case study reported a flexible cancellation policy lifted bookings by 26 percent and revenue by 53 percent per listing [https://partner.expediagroup.com/en-us/resources/case-studies/evolve-drives-success-with-flexible-cancellation-policies]. It covers rentals, not guided trips. Treat as a hypothesis to test.

Row 17: Thaler (1985) on mental accounting [Marketing Science 4(3): https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:4:y:1985:i:3:p:199-214].

Row 20: Practitioner rule. The legal side is in section 5.

Rows 21, 25, 26: Berger and Milkman (2012) analysed 6,956 New York Times articles from three months of 2008. For a one standard deviation increase, the probability of reaching the most-emailed list rose by 30 percent for awe and 30 percent for practical value [https://jonahberger.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/ViralityB.pdf]. Concrete language: Hu, Pilgrim, Zhao and Hills (2025) found concreteness predicted sharing in 15.5 million Twitter posts, 50,517 Reddit posts and an experiment with 167 participants [https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13062457/]. Hansen and Wänke (2010) found concrete wording was judged more likely true across four experiments [https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167210386238]. News emails and posts are not ads, so read the effect sizes as direction only.

Row 22: Loewenstein (1994) information-gap theory. Curiosity needs some prior knowledge plus a visible missing piece [secondary summary: https://www.ignorancegraph.com/information-gaps/information-gap-theory/]. The primary paper was only available as an unreadable scan, so do not quote a size relationship [VERIFY: primary text]. Meta can treat clickbait as a policy problem, so the payoff must be real.

Rows 23, 24: Heath brothers on stories and concreteness [https://heathbrothers.com/made-to-stick-introduction/]. Cialdini on Pre-Suasion (2016). Practitioner translation. Grade C.

Row 27: Berger and Ward (2010) showed insiders prefer subtle signals that outsiders miss [Journal of Consumer Research 37(4): https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/37/4/555/1795043].

Rows 28, 29: Tversky and Kahneman (1981) framing [Science 211: https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.7455683]. Gilovich and Medvec (1995) found people regret inaction more in the long run [https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-experience-of-regret:-what,-when,-and-why.-Gilovich-Medvec/7792f8a0a372c01f572b326315ee9739df7e40b2]. A replication appeared in Collabra in 2022, but the notes could not open the result [VERIFY: replication outcome].

Rows 30, 31: Kumar, Killingsworth and Gilovich (2014) found people were happier thinking about an upcoming experiential purchase, with experience sampling of 2,266 adults [summary: https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/08/140825100051.htm]. Van Boven and Gilovich (2003) found experiential purchases were judged to make people happier than material ones [https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/To-do-or-to-have-That-is-the-question.-Boven-Gilovich/85844562e67d74f1b134ce9ad86f7650d55e5436]. Nawijn et al (2010) found vacationers were happier before the trip and most were not happier after [https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/vacationers-happier-but-most-not-happier-after-a-holiday/]. The honest reading for copy: the weeks before count too, and a week on snow does not change a life. Never promise lasting happiness.

Rows 32, 33, 34: Moesta's four forces of progress: push, pull, anxiety, habit [https://jobstobedone.org/radio/unpacking-the-progress-making-forces-diagram/]. A practitioner model built from interviews, not a lab result. Grade C, high value as a copy tool.

Rows 35, 36, 37: Ryan and Deci (2000) self-determination theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness [https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf]. Peer reviewed theory with no ad-copy test in the notes. Grade C for the copy move.

Row 38: Fogg's behaviour model says behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt meet at the same time [https://www.behaviormodel.org/]. A design checklist with little formal test.

Row 39: Practitioner pattern from group decision research in tourism, which is general and not ski-specific [VERIFY: no ski-specific peer reviewed study of the second reader was found].

Row 40: Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010) meta-analysed 63 conditions from 50 experiments (N = 5,036) and found the mean choice overload effect "virtually zero" with high variance [https://scheibehenne.com/ScheibehenneGreifenederTodd2010.pdf]. Chernev, Bockenholt and Goodman (2015) name set complexity, decision difficulty, preference uncertainty and decision goal as moderators [https://chernev.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ChoiceOverload_JCP_2015.pdf]. A first-time buyer of a high-ticket trip fits the conditions where overload can bite. Graded B for the single-trip move because the evidence is conditional.

## 5. Ethics rules: true scarcity only, no manufactured urgency, no dark patterns

These rules override every row above. A line that breaks one is cut, however well it would test.

1. True scarcity only. A number comes from the booking system on the day of publishing, or it is a fixed fact such as "eight guests per departure".
2. No manufactured urgency. No restarting timers, no invented deadlines, no "only 2 left" that nobody checked. The skill writes no countdown at all.
3. No dark patterns. Hidden fees, pre-ticked extras, confirm-shaming ("No thanks, I hate adventure"), obstructed cancellation and false hierarchy between buttons all stay out of the copy and out of any landing page advice.
4. No pressure on vulnerable states such as grief, loneliness or worry about health.
5. No claim about guests that you cannot document: reviews, ratings, numbers, outcomes.
6. Evidence for a factual claim has to exist when the ad runs. The Norwegian Marketing Control Act section 3 expects documentation of factual claims [https://lovdata.no/dokument/NLE/lov/2009-01-09-2] [VERIFY with counsel].
7. The mechanism test: if the guest would feel tricked on seeing how the line was built, drop it.
8. The skill refuses to invent proof. When a ledger row for a review, rating, award, count or deadline is empty, it writes the ad without that line and says what is missing.

Why these rules have teeth beyond taste. Falsely claiming that an offer is about to end, to force a quick decision, is on the EU list of practices banned in all circumstances [https://eur-lex.europa.eu/NL/legal-content/summary/unfair-commercial-practices.html]. Forbrukertilsynet's subdirector said in 2023 that an automatically restarting countdown timer "will easily be a breach of the marketing law" [https://netthandel.no/forbrukertilsynet-forklarer-dette-ma-nettbutikkene-unnga-a-gjore/]. In 2019 the UK CMA obtained binding commitments from six booking sites on pressure-selling claims, including the number of rooms left [https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/CMA-secures-commitments-from-holiday-booking-sites-on-sales-practices]. None of this is legal advice. Check price claims, urgency, reviews and package travel information with counsel [VERIFY with counsel].

## 6. Levers with weak or contested evidence

The behavioural science canon is uneven. Treat these as hypotheses, never as laws.

- Loss aversion. Kahneman and Tversky's cumulative estimate of about 2.25 [https://repub.eur.nl/pub/10951/LossAversionunderProspect.pdf] is contested. Gal and Rucker (2018) concluded the evidence does not support the general claim that losses loom larger than gains [https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jcpy.1047], and Simonson and Kivetz replied with a contingent version [https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jcpy.1046]. Loss framing can be tested in specific contexts. Never put a fixed multiplier in copy logic and never quote 2.25 in output.
- Decoy (asymmetric dominance). Frederick, Lee and Baskin (2014) found the effect largely vanishes with realistic, non-numeric options [VERIFY: source URL not opened in the notes]. Skip decoy pricing for trip tiers.
- Choice overload. See row 40. The mean effect is near zero, and it shows up when options are many, similar and the buyer is unsure.
- Scarcity. Weakest lever in Google's test, and a legal risk when faked. Use only when true and late in the path.
- Dan Ariely. A 2012 honesty-pledge paper he co-authored was retracted in 2021 after the insurance data were shown to be fabricated [https://bigthink.com/the-present/honesty-pledge-study-fraud-dan-ariely/]. Retraction Watch reports a 2002 Psychological Science paper on procrastination and deadlines was retracted on 2 September 2026 [https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/03/procrastination-study-duke-dan-ariely-psychological-science-data-colada-tampering-retraction/]. Use his concepts only where independent evidence exists, such as Google's power-of-free result.
- Dwell time. The idea that a thumb moves past a feed ad in a second or two has no source in the notes [VERIFY: no source opened]. Do not print a number of seconds in any output.

## 7. Worked example: weak, then strong

Fictional brief: Brekke Ski Guides, six day hut-to-hut trip, 14 and 21 Mar 2027, NOK 18,500, Meta feed. The first draft below is what a default reply tends to produce. It is wrapped in lint ignore markers because it contains the patterns this skill bans.

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->

Weak primary text: "Only 3 spots left! Book NOW for the adventure of a lifetime. Thousands of happy skiers. Worried you are too unfit? Our amazing, unforgettable tour has it all."

What went wrong, row by row:

- "Only 3 spots left" has no ledger row. It is a fake scarcity claim (rows 18 to 20, ethics rule 1).
- "Thousands of happy skiers" has no count, source or date (rows 1 and 2).
- "Worried you are too unfit?" addresses a health attribute of the viewer. Meta's personal attributes rule is the reason to cut it (policy: https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/, checked 2026-10-06, confirm in the live policy).

## 8. Pre-flight checklist

Run this as the last step of every generation. Print pass or fail per line in the working notes, never in the ad. Fail closed: if line 9 or 10 cannot be answered, hold the ad back.

1. Stage: trigger, consideration, doubt or decision. One only.
2. Force: which of push, pull, anxiety or habit does this ad address?
3. Lead lever: one row from the table, with its grade.
4. Concreteness: at least one named place, number, time or sensation.
5. Proof: a real ledger source is present, or the proof line is dropped and the gap is reported.
6. Total price present, with the inclusions or a link to them.
7. Second reader pass: do the what, when and how much questions survive a paste into a blank chat?
8. Personal attribute scan on Meta: no "you" next to a health, age, disability, financial, relationship or identity word.
9. Scarcity and urgency scan: every number or date comes with a source and a date.
10. Reduction scan: any "was and now" or percentage-off claim has the prior price and its dates [VERIFY with counsel].
11. Style scan: run scripts/slop_lint.py and fix block and warn hits.
12. Honesty: would the operator be comfortable if the guest saw how the line was built?
