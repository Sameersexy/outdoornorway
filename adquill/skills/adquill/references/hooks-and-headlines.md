# Hooks, headlines and CTAs: typology, scorecard, the 25 line grid, mobile line craft

What it is: how to write the first thing a reader meets (image line, first line of primary text, first three seconds of a Reel, first line of an X post), how to score it, and how to write the call to action. Read it at method step 5 (write to slots) and whenever the user asks for variants, a new angle or a stronger headline.

Every example uses the invented company Brekke Ski Guides. Every example is a fictional brief. Prices, dates, group sizes, ratings and places are invented for teaching and make no claim about any real operator.

## 1. What a first line has to do

A first line has about one second. It does four jobs.

1. Hail the right reader. Hopkins: the first thing you say is "Hey there, Bill Jones" to get the right person's attention [PRIMARY: Scientific Advertising, chapter 5, https://archive.org/details/scientific-advertising].
2. Earn the next line. Sugarman's version: the only job of the first sentence is to get the second sentence read [SECONDARY: https://www.creative-copywriter.net/content-marketing/learning-from-joseph-sugarman].
3. Carry one specific. A number, a date or a named thing.
4. Survive truncation. On the image and in the text, the line must still work when cut.

Run these four as a checklist on every hook, then run the last test: read the line with the image hidden and the sound off. If it still tells a stranger what is on offer, it passes.

Hopkins on why it matters: readers "skip three-fourths of the reading matter which they pay to get" [PRIMARY: chapter 5]. The speed has changed since 1923. The habit has not.

## 3. The hook typology

Eight hook types, each with a weak and a strong example, and the reason. The taxonomy comes from practitioner guides, not from any standard [SECONDARY: https://www.opus.pro/blog/instagram-reels-hook-formulas and https://www.heyorca.com/blog/the-best-social-media-hooks-for-2026, vendor blogs]. Use it as a menu for diversity. Label every headline with its type so the user can see what is being tested.

Which type for which awareness level is in craft.md, section 2. A short version: unaware and problem aware readers like story, question and pattern interrupt. Solution aware readers like specificity and mechanism. Product aware readers like proof and the honest concession. Most aware readers like the plain offer.

All of the following is a fictional brief.

### 3.1 Direct benefit

States what the reader gets.

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->
- Weak: "Experience the mountains like never before."
<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-end -->
- Strong: "Climb a Norwegian summit on skis. One guided Saturday, NOK 2,350." (65 characters)
- Why: the weak line could sit on any outdoor brand's page. The strong line names the result, the guide, the day and the price. A reader can picture the summit.
- Best at: problem aware and most aware readers.
- Watch for: promise only what the ledger supports. Never promise conditions, snow or a summit.
- Real precedent: Hopkins' own hypothetical, that a soap line saying "it floats" might prove interesting. It is his thought experiment in chapter 5 and not a documented ad [PRIMARY].

### 3.2 Specificity

An exact number proves effort.

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->
- Weak: "Small groups and expert guides."
<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-end -->
- Strong: "Six guests per guide. Never seven." (34 characters)
- Why: the number can be checked. "Never seven" is a commitment, and a commitment is a fact a competitor has to match.
- Best at: solution aware and product aware readers.
- Watch for: a number that does not matter ("37 peaks") is noise.
- Real precedent: Hopkins cites a razor maker advertising a "78-second shave" [PRIMARY: chapter 7]. The Volkswagen "Lemon" ad (1960) is built on numbers: 3,389 inspectors, one car in fifty rejected [SECONDARY: transcription at https://swipefile.com/volkswagen-lemon-print-ad/lemon-ad, VERIFY against a scan].

### 3.3 Curiosity

Opens a gap the reader needs to close. Use it only when the next line pays it off.

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->
- Weak: "You will never guess what we found above the fjord."
<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-end -->
- Strong: "Two kinds of ski days. One weekend." (35 characters), followed in the text by "Saturday on a slope we have checked. Sunday on a summit you choose."
- Why: the gap closes inside one line. The weak version opens a gap and has nothing to put in it, which reads as bait.
- Best at: unaware and solution aware readers, when the payoff is a real fact.
- Watch for: the promise test. Dry says a good title is one where the reader would feel cheated if the content broke the promise [PRIMARY: https://marketingexamples.com/copywriting/promise]. X's deceptive content policy targets clickbait with sensational language [VERIFY: policy page seen only as a summary].
- Real precedent: "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano... But When I Started to Play!" by John Caples for the U.S. School of Music, written in 1926 (some sources say 1925) [SECONDARY, VERIFY: https://archive.seattletimes.com/archive/19900611/1076601/john-caples-author-of-famous-ad-line]. No sales figures appear in the sources read.

### 3.4 Contrarian

Opposes the usual advice or the usual promise.

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->
- Weak: "Forget everything you know about skiing."
<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-end -->
- Strong: "Three hours up for twenty minutes down." (39 characters)
- Why: it states the honest cost of the product, it pushes against the speed culture of resort skiing, and it lets the reader pick themselves in or out. The weak line contradicts nothing in particular.
- Best at: solution aware and product aware readers in a crowded category.
- Watch for: the contrary claim must be true. Check each against the ledger.
- Real precedent: Patagonia's "Don't Buy This Jacket" ad in the New York Times on Black Friday, 25 November 2011 [SECONDARY, VERIFY: date and every figure quoted about its effect come from company statements and retellings. Do not repeat a sales figure].

### 3.5 Story

A person, a turn, a payoff.

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->
- Weak: "Our guests love it!"
<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-end -->
- Strong: "We capped groups at six in 2016. We never changed it." (53 characters)
- Why: a date, a decision and a fact that still holds. It comes from the ledger, so it is true.
- Best at: unaware readers, and anyone who needs a reason to trust a small company.
- Watch for: a story hook needs a real story. Never invent a guest, a name or a quote. Use the company's own history, or a quote the guest allowed, with the source type named ("a guest review on Google").
- Real precedent: the Caples piano ad above is a story in one headline.

### 3.6 Proof

Visible evidence.

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->
- Weak: "Trusted by hundreds of skiers."
<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-end -->
<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->
- Strong: "Rated 4.8 by 112 guests on Google (checked 1 Oct 2026)" (54 characters)
<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-end -->
- Why: the strong line gives a rating, a count, a platform and a date, so anyone can check it. The weak line is a claim with nothing behind it.
- The numbers above are invented for this example. In a real brief the user supplies the rating, the count, the platform and the date. adquill never invents proof. If the user has none, the skill writes a different hook and says why.
- Best at: product aware readers.
- Watch for: Meta's policy summaries say testimonials must represent a typical experience and unsupported statistics get flagged [SECONDARY: https://www.auditsocials.com/blog/meta-ad-misleading-claims-personal-attributes-prohibited-content-policy-2026, VERIFY against Meta's live page]. See policy.md.
- Real precedent: Ogilvy's Rolls-Royce line, "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock." The fact implies the benefit and nobody has to say "quiet" [SECONDARY: https://www.enchantingmarketing.com/david-ogilvy-rolls-royce-advert/, VERIFY the date].

### 3.7 Question

Asks what the reader is already asking.

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->
- Weak: "Ready for an adventure?"
<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-end -->
- Strong: "Never ski toured? Start with one Saturday." (42 characters)
- Why: it hails a specific person (a skier who has never toured) by an activity, and it gives a first step instead of a leap.
- Best at: problem aware readers.
- Watch for: a question about an activity is fine. A question that states or implies a personal trait is a policy problem on Meta ("Are you out of shape?"). See policy.md.
- Real precedent: "Do You Make These Mistakes in English?", the Sherwin Cody ad credited to Max Sackheim, said to have run for roughly 40 to 45 years [SECONDARY, VERIFY: sources disagree on the length].

### 3.8 Pattern interrupt

Breaks the expected shape of an ad. Most of the break is visual, so a text skill suggests it to the designer and does not fake it in words.

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->
- Weak: a glossy sunrise photo with "Adventure awaits."
<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-end -->
- Strong: a plain screenshot of the regional avalanche forecast with the line "We read this before every trip. It picks the route." (51 characters)
- Why: the plain look does not resemble an ad, and the line tells the truth about how the trip is run. It is also a mechanism (see craft.md, section 8).
- Best at: unaware readers.
- Watch for: the screenshot must be real and current. Do not stage it.
- Real precedent: REI's #OptOutside in 2015, when the company closed its stores on Black Friday [SECONDARY, VERIFY: https://www.rei.com/blog/social/the-history-of-opt-outside, seen as a search snippet. The store and participant counts are the company's own]. Harry Dry argues for unpolished ads on his site, and his dollar figure for one example is unverified [PRIMARY page, claim VERIFY: https://marketingexamples.com/ads/ugly].

### 3.9 Rules for all eight

- One hook per ad. Do not stack a question on top of a number on top of a story.
- The hook never promises what the next line fails to deliver.
- Avoid the stock contrast frame. It is overused in machine text, and one honest line does the work.
- Variants for the same offer must differ in hook type and angle. Synonym swaps are not variants.

## 4. Caples' headline families

John Caples, *Tested Advertising Methods*, catalogued about 35 headline formulas in families [SECONDARY: https://larslofgren.com/john-caples-headline-formulas/]. The page names the families and gives no examples, and the book's first edition year is reported as 1930 or 1932 [VERIFY]. The Brekke examples below are adquill's own. Showing the family next to each headline makes the output teachable.

| Family | Pattern | Fictional brief example |
|---|---|---|
| Keyword openers | How to, Why, Who else, Which, If, Because | "How to ski your first summit" (28 characters) |
| Keyword openers | Why | "Why we cap groups at six" (24 characters) |
| Keyword openers | If | "If you ski red runs, you can start here." (40 characters) |
| Benefit | Specific audience | "Never ski toured? Start with one Saturday." (42 characters) |
| News | Date inclusion, New, Now | "New for 2027: a six day hut trip" (32 characters) |
| Price | Price feature | "Two ski days for NOK 4,900" (26 characters) |
| One to three words | Short | "Six guests." (11 characters) |

Caples' own tone: blunt beats beautiful. His line, as passed on by a teaching page, is that what you say counts more than how you say it [SECONDARY: https://maverickwords.com/4-copywriting-lessons-from-john-caples-tested-advertising-methods].

"New for 2027" needs a ledger fact that the trip is new. Do not use "New" because it sounds good.

## 5. The headline scorecard

adquill's scorecard is a synthesis of the 4Us (Useful, Urgent, Unique, Ultra-specific, credited to Michael Masterson and AWAI [SECONDARY: https://www.awai.com/2022/05/4-goof-proof-ways-to-write-irresistible-headlines/]), Hopkins, Ogilvy and Dry. It is not a published standard. Score each line 0 to 2. Keep anything at 9 or more out of 14. Tune the cutoff on real results.

How to score: 0 means absent, 1 means partial, 2 means clear.

| # | Test | Question to ask | Source behind it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hail | Does it name or imply the person: level, group, moment? | Hopkins [PRIMARY] |
| 2 | Specific | Does it contain one exact number, date or named thing? (4Us: Ultra-specific) | Hopkins [PRIMARY], Dry [SECONDARY] |
| 3 | Picture | Can you see it in your head? | Dry [SECONDARY: https://www.upgrow.io/harry-dry-copywriting-3-rules/] |
| 4 | Only us | Could only this brand say it? (4Us: Unique) | Dry, Trott [SECONDARY] |
| 5 | Benefit | Does it carry a benefit, news or a reason? (4Us: Useful) | Caples, Ogilvy [SECONDARY] |
| 6 | Urgency | 2 for real, dated urgency. 1 for none. 0 for fake urgency. (4Us: Urgent) | 4Us, Kennedy [SECONDARY] |
| 7 | Fit | Does it fit its slot and pass the house style? 2 fits as is, 1 fits after a trim, 0 does not | adquill |

Urgency is the one the machine abuses. A line with no urgency scores 1, which is neutral and fine. A line with invented urgency scores 0 and fails the next gate whatever else it scores.

Hard gates that stop a line before scoring:

- A banned word, a dash character, an emoji or capitals used for emphasis.
- A number, date or claim not in the fact ledger.
- Fake urgency or fake scarcity.
- A policy block-tier phrase (see policy.md).
- Over the slot budget with no honest trim.

Checklist form, for the final pick:

- [ ] Names the reader
- [ ] One exact number, date or named thing from the ledger
- [ ] I can picture it
- [ ] A competitor could not copy it word for word
- [ ] A benefit, news or a reason is in the line
- [ ] Urgency is real and dated, or absent
- [ ] Fits the slot and the style
- [ ] Reads true with the image hidden and the sound off

Worked scoring, fictional brief. Candidate: "Six guests per guide. Never seven." Hail 1 (a skier who wants a small group). Specific 2. Picture 1. Only us 2. Benefit 2 (the reason is built in). Urgency 1. Fit 1 (34 characters, over the 27 headline budget, fits after a trim). Total 10 out of 14. Trimmed for the headline slot: "Six guests per guide." (21 characters). The trim loses "never seven", which moves to image line 2.

## 6. The 25 line grid

The setup takes about 30 seconds. The writing takes a few minutes for a person and one pass for a model.

1. Pick five facts from the ledger. Each is a number, a date, a named thing, a constraint or the price. (10 seconds)
2. Pick five hook types that fit the awareness level. (5 seconds)
3. Draw a grid, facts down the side, hook types across the top. Write one line per cell, seven words or fewer. If a cell has no natural line, leave it empty. Do not force it. (15 seconds)
4. Cut. Delete every line with a banned word, a dash, an emoji, a stock contrast frame, an unverified fact or fake urgency.
5. Score the survivors with the scorecard. Keep the five best that differ in hook type. If fewer than five clear the cutoff, keep the best of each type and say so.
6. Pick one. Fit it to its slot. Keep two others as variants, chosen so that their hook types differ from the winner's.

Fictional brief. Offer: weekend course. Ledger facts used: F1 price NOK 4,900 for two days. F2 six guests at most per guide. F3 skins, shovel, probe and transceiver lent. F4 Sunnmore Alps, Saturdays and Sundays, January to April 2027. F5 you must be comfortable on red runs at a resort. Awareness: problem aware. Hook types: benefit, number, question, concession, scene.

| Fact | Direct benefit | Number | Question | Concession | Scene |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| F1 price | Two guided ski days. NOK 4,900. | NOK 4,900 buys two days on skins. | What does NOK 4,900 buy on a mountain? | Not cheap. NOK 4,900 for two days. | Two days off the lift. NOK 4,900. |
| F2 group | A guide for every six skiers. | 6 guests. 1 guide. Every trip. | Want a guide who knows your name? | Small groups cost more. Ours has six. | Six of you, one guide, a white ridge. |
| F3 kit | Skins and avalanche kit are lent. | Shovel, probe, transceiver: lent. | No avalanche kit? You can still come. | You still bring your own skis. | Shovel. Probe. Transceiver. Lent. |
| F4 place and dates | Saturdays and Sundays, Jan to Apr. | Sunnmore Alps. Jan to Apr. Weekends. | Free weekend in the Sunnmore Alps? | Weekends only, January to April. | Sunnmore Alps. A Saturday. Skins on. |
| F5 level | Ski touring for people who like red runs. | Red run skiers: this one fits. | Comfortable on red runs? Come up. | If red runs scare you, wait a year. | Red run Saturday, summit Sunday. |

Every line in the grid is 29 to 41 characters, so the grid is a pool for the hook and not yet a set of slot lines.

Step 4 cuts. "A white ridge" is a scene the ledger does not support, so it goes. "If red runs scare you, wait a year" sits close to a personal-attribute line, so it goes until policy.md clears it. "Want a guide who knows your name?" promises a relationship the ledger does not mention, so it goes.

Step 5 scores. Five survivors, scored on the seven tests. Fit is scored against the 27 character headline budget, so a line of 28 to 41 characters scores 1 until it is trimmed.

| Line | Hail | Spec | Pic | Only us | Ben | Urg | Fit | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| "6 guests. 1 guide. Every trip." | 1 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 10 |
| "A guide for every six skiers." | 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 9 |
| "Comfortable on red runs? Come up." | 2 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 6 |
| "Not cheap. NOK 4,900 for two days." | 0 | 2 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 6 |
| "Skins and avalanche kit are lent." | 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 6 |

Two lines clear the cutoff of 9. When fewer than five clear it, take the best line of each remaining hook type and rewrite it to close its gaps. Say so in the output.

Step 6. The winner is "6 guests. 1 guide. Every trip." (30 characters, three over the 27 headline budget). Trim it to "Six guests. One guide." (22 characters) for the headline. The trim loses nothing. For variants, the question line has no number, so rewrite it to "Comfortable on red runs? Two days, NOK 4,900." (45 characters) and rescore. The concession line stays as a third angle. Each variant is its own ad with its own image.

The scores are a worked illustration with invented ledger facts. They show the procedure. They do not show that this line would win in a real test.

## 7. CTA writing rules

There is little hard evidence on CTA wording. What follows is craft doctrine and platform behaviour. Say so when you present it, and mark every CTA as a test candidate, never a proven winner.

The doctrine:

- Hopkins: "The best ads ask no one to buy." The best ones offer wanted information and let the reader prove the claims at no cost or risk [PRIMARY: chapter 3]. A feed ad has a button, so the copy does not need to say "Buy now". Use the line near the button to remove one doubt.
- Kennedy: one clear next step [SECONDARY: https://hooshmand.net/no-bs-direct-marketing-dan-kennedy-summary/].
- Guidance for X: put a clear CTA in the ad [SECONDARY, VERIFY: https://www.jeffbullas.com/thread/what-are-the-best-practices-when-advertising-on-x/].

The rules:

1. One action per ad.
2. The verb predicts the next step. The reader should be able to say what will happen after the tap. "See dates" leads to dates. "Check availability" leads to a calendar. "Book now" leads to a booking form.
3. The label names the next step and not the purchase, unless the landing page is a booking form.
4. Put the reason next to the button. "Two departures only" is a good line if the ledger lists two departures.
5. No urgency wording unless the urgency is true, dated and in the ledger.
6. The button label comes from Meta's list, so the CTA you write has two parts: the button label and the line beside it.
7. Match the commitment to the awareness level. A cold audience gets a low-commitment verb. A most aware audience gets a high one.

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->
Why "Explore" is weak. The verb names no next step, so the reader cannot predict what the tap does. It has no object. It invites an idle click that costs money and qualifies nobody. It also does not appear on Meta's feed button list as read on 2026-10-06 (31 labels for the Facebook feed image ad, 27 for Instagram feed, source: https://www.facebook.com/business/ads-guide/update/image/facebook-feed/link-clicks). If you write "Explore the tour" in a headline, the button still has to be a label from the list, and the two should not say different things.
<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-end -->

Meta's own caveat: not every button is available to every advertiser, and the list varies by industry, objective and conversion location. meta.md carries the current list and the travel subset. Check Ads Manager before you recommend a label.

Three CTA lines per ad, as test candidates, fictional brief (six day trip, landing page shows two departures and a booking form):

| Commitment | Line beside the button | Button label | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | "See both departures" (19 characters) | Learn more | Cold or problem aware audiences |
| Medium | "Pick 14 or 21 Mar" (17 characters) | Check Availability | Product aware audiences |
| High | "Hold a place" (12 characters) | Book now | Most aware audiences, landing page with a booking form |

Recommend one and say why. On X, the post carries the link and the line before it does the work. The link goes last. The button rules for X website cards follow X's own list [VERIFY: current website card labels, see x.md].

## 8. First line and line break craft for mobile

The doctrine comes from Dry (more periods, fewer commas, think slippery slide, and treat the first line as the one that matters most [PRIMARY: https://marketingexamples.com/copywriting/tips]) and Sugarman (vary sentence length, simple words [SECONDARY]). No controlled study of line breaks in Meta primary text turned up. Test them.

Rules:

1. One thought per line. A line is a complete beat.
2. Put the number alone on a line when the number is the point.
3. Keep lines short. A UX source suggests 30 to 50 characters per line on a phone held upright [SECONDARY: https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/optimal-line-length-for-readability/, VERIFY: general readability, not ad specific]. Use it as a rule of thumb for on-image text.
4. The "See more" cut is set by the layout, so a hard line break can push the hook off the visible area [VERIFY on a real device]. Check the preview.
5. Avoid the drumbeat: three sentences of equal length, repeated triplets, stacked colon reveals. Vary length.
6. No emoji and no dashes. A line such as "Saturday. Norway. NOK 2,350." can carry rhythm with periods alone.
7. No sentence appears in two slots. If the image says it, the text adds the reason.

Weak and strong primary text, fictional brief (weekend course):

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-start -->
Weak (125 characters):

"Looking for a winter adventure? Join our ski touring weekend in the Sunnmore Alps with guides who care about your experience."

<!-- adquill-lint: ignore-end -->
Why it fails: the first 40 characters hail nobody. Nothing is specific. "Guides who care" is a claim with no number behind it. There is no price and no date.

Strong (99 characters, line breaks counted as one character each):

Six guests. One guide.
Two days of ski touring in the Sunnmore Alps.
NOK 4,900. Avalanche kit lent.

Why it works: line one is 22 characters and can stand alone. Line two says what. Line three says how much and removes one worry. The whole block fits inside the 125 visible characters.

X post, same offer (a native post, 280 characters at most, written to stand with no image):

Never ski toured?

There is a guided weekend for that. Six guests per guide, January to April 2027.

NOK 4,900. brekkeskiguides.example/weekend

The line break rhythm is one beat per line, the link comes last, and the fact sits in line one or two. Count characters in code. X shortens links in a fixed way [VERIFY: current rule].

## 9. Writing for sound off and for skimmers

Sound off.

- Meta says captioned video ads raised view time by an average of 12 percent in its internal tests, and that 41 percent of videos in one Facebook video ad study were basically meaningless without sound [PRIMARY: https://www.facebook.com/business/news/updated-features-for-video-ads, VERIFY: undated, studies not named].
- Do not repeat the claim that 85 percent of Facebook video is watched without sound. It comes from a Digiday story of 17 May 2016 quoting publishers, about their own video, and many later blogs misattribute it to Meta [SECONDARY: https://www.niemanlab.org/reading/publishers-say-85-percent-of-facebook-video-is-watched-without-sound/, VERIFY]. Use the design rule and not the percentage: design for sound off, then add sound as a bonus.

Craft rules for video and Reels text:

1. The offer and the number are in on-screen text.
2. Burned-in captions in the brand font.
3. The hook is readable in the first half second.
4. Never rely on a voiceover line for price or date.
5. Keep text out of the Stories and Reels safe zone: 14 percent from the top, 35 percent from the bottom and 6 percent on each side [VERIFY: Meta's help page did not load for the research team. See meta.md for the figures with their source].

Skimmers.

Make the ad work at three depths:

1. Image plus headline. About two seconds.
2. The first line of primary text. About five seconds.
3. The full text, for the few who tap "See more". The claim that about 1 percent tap it is a vendor figure with no source [VERIFY].

Put checkable facts where a skimmer's eye lands: a number, a date, a price, each on its own line.

Reading level. Vendors recommend grades 5 to 7 and sentences of 8 to 12 words. One study found posts readable at grade 5 or 6 earned more likes [SECONDARY: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328295826_How_Readability_Shapes_Social_Media_Engagement, VERIFY: platform and period unknown]. Another study warns readability formulas are unreliable on short text. Use plain words, and test the result on a person.
